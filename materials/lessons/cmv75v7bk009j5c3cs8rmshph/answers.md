# Worked answers

All data here are fictional Academy fixtures. Expected values below are calculations and authored chart descriptions, not observed app output.

1. The truncated card can suggest a dramatic rise. Its title is Weekly website visits, categories Week 1 and Week 2, counts in visits, axis 94–101 in unit steps. Plotted values are 96 and 100. Visible segments are 2 and 6 units above 94: the latter segment is three times as long, but the count is only 100/96 = 1.0416667 times as large. The difference is 4 visits; relative increase is 4/96 × 100 = 4.1666667%, about 4.17% (or 4.2% to one decimal). Revised claim: Week 2 recorded 4 more visits, about 4.17% above Week 1. It did not double.
2. First Week 1=96, middle Week 2=100 and Week 3=98, last Week 4=73. Weeks 1–3 omit the decline in Week 4. A1:B4 contains the header plus three data rows; A1:B5 includes all four. From Week 3 to 4 the difference is -25 visits and (-25/98)*100 = -25.5102041%, about a 25.51% decrease. This does not establish a cause for the decline.
3. D2's ratio is 0.0416666667, displayed as 4.17% when percentage formatting uses two decimals. Multiplying by 100 again in a percent-formatted cell would wrongly display 416.67%. A zero baseline displays nearly equal bars; it restores quantity-proportional lengths. Zero is usually required for magnitude-encoding bars; restricted line-chart axes can be justified by a clearly labelled detail question.
4. A1:B4 ends at Week 3=98. A1:B5 ends at Week 4=73. Compare labels as well as values to detect mismatched category/series selections. An incomplete selected range can be plotted accurately and still omit relevant evidence.
5. Both monthly totals equal 100. June is Morning 90 + Evening 10; July is Morning 50 + Evening 50. Morning changed by -40 and evening by +40, while total changed by 0. Evening share rose from 10% to 50%, a 40 percentage-point increase. The total answers an overall-count question; staffing by session requires subgroup counts. This fixture has no blank or missing records; no claim is made about data outside it. No daily records, means, medians or rates are supplied.
6. a) equal linear increments; b) increments 10,40,10 on equal spacing, inconsistent with a regular linear axis unless another explicit scale explains it; c) reversed axis; d) factor-ten increments consistent with a logarithmic axis, requiring confirmation. A break omits part of a range and must be visible and explained. Apparent decorative volume is not a dependable value measure; return to labelled values or the table.
7. Cropping can hide the title, baseline, period, units, notes and source. Ask for the uncropped chart and underlying numbers. Missing information warrants a provisional reading, never invented labels or values.
8. Useful questions ask what each axis measures, which rows were used, what aggregation occurred and what information is missing. A confident accuracy assertion from an image alone is insufficient verification. Actual assistant wording will vary; the kit contains no fabricated response.

Manual fallback: calculate 100-96=4, then 4/96*100≈4.17. Sketch bars from zero with Week 1 about 96% the height of Week 2; add Week 3 and Week 4, label all values. For aggregation, write the two sums and compare morning/evening columns. If the complete chart or table cannot be obtained, record the exact unknowns and withhold a final numerical verdict.
